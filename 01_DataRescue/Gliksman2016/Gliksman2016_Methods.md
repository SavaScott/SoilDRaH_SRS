# Materials and methods

## Field sites

The first Mediterranean site is located on Carmel Ridge in Israel (Ramat Hanadiv Nature Park; 32°30′N, 34°550′E; Figs S1 and S2), ~4 km from the Mediterranean coast at 120 m above sea level (asl).
The climate is characterized by mild and moist winters (main rainy season between December and February), and hot and dry summers, with a rainless period between May and October.
Mean annual precipitation is 534 mm (19-year average; www.ramathanadiv-edu.org.il/weather.asp), and mean annual temperature is 21.0 °C (10-year average).
Vegetation is a maquis-type shrubland dominated by winter annuals, the summer-deciduous leguminous shrub *Calicotome villosa* (Poir.) Link, and evergreen shrubs [@Dirks2010].
The substrate is characterized by hard limestone covered by red brown Terra rossa soil.
The site was selected (i) to conduct a microclimate manipulation experiment that affected solar irradiance, nighttime temperature and humidity during the dry season, and (ii) to serve as one of three trans-Mediterranean sites where we conducted Flitter measurements at the annual peak of solar irradiance (Fig. S1).
The second site is part of the EU INCREASE integrated research network, located on the peninsula of Capo Caccia (northwestern Sardinia, Italy), within the Regional Park Porto Conte (40°37′N, 8°10′E), ~4 km from the Mediterranean coast at 40 m asl.
The climate is characterized by mild and moist winters (main rainy season between December and February), and hot and dry summers, with a relatively rainless period between May and August. Mean annual precipitation is 640 mm [@deDato2013].
Vegetation is an ungrazed maquis dominated by small evergreen and deciduous shrubs, such as *Cistus monspeliensis* (L.), *Daphne gnidium* (L.), *Pistacia lentiscus* (L.), *Dorycnium pentaphyllum* [subsp. *amani* (Zohary) Ponert], and *Helichrysum italicum* (G. Don f.).
The soil is classified as a shallow Terra rossa.
The third site is located to the southwest of Madrid, near the village of Chapinería (40°23′N 4°11′W) at 670 m asl.
The continental Mediterranean climate has a mean annual temperature of 12 °C and a mean annual precipitation of 433 mm.
The vegetation is a woodland of *Quercus ilex* ssp. *ballota*, with the presence of *Juniperus communis, Retama* spp., and winter annuals.
Soil texture is sandy to sandy loam upon fractured bedrock of granite.

##  Microclimate manipulation experiment (Israel)

To investigate the impact of microclimatic factors on F$_{litter}$ and decomposition, we applied the following three field treatments at the site in Israel (Fig. S2): (i) ambient solar radiation, temperature, and humidity (no screens or frames, ‘no-screen’ control treatment); (ii) near-ambient solar radiation under transparent screens (4000TR; Honeywell International, Morristown, NJ, USA), which led to warming and drying during the night (‘transparent’ treatment); and (iii) solar UV and shortwave PAR (up to a 550 nm) blocked by 179 Chrome orange filters (Lee Filters, Burbank, CA, USA; @Brandt2009) which led also to warming and drying during nights (‘radiation-block’ treatment).
Each screen was mounted on a white aluminum double frame (1.22 × 1.22 m), suspended 0.35 m above the litterbags at the northern edge and 0.30 m at the southern edge.
Tilting the frames allowed dew to drain.
Five no-screen control replicates of 1.2 × 1.2 m, five transparent screens and five radiation-block screens were randomly distributed in the study area of 1000 m$^2$.
Radiation screens were deployed for 108 days, only during the dry season (June–September 2012) and were exchanged once, 54 days after deployment.

Fresh, standing dead material was harvested from the annual species *Avena sterilis* L. and *Scabiosa prolifera* L., and naturally shed litter was collected from nets placed under the leguminous, summer-deciduous shrub *C. villosa* [(Poir.) Link.], all dominant species at the site.
These species differed in litter quality, in particular in C:N ratios (120.1, 20.8 and 148.2 for Avena, Calicotome and Scabiosa), lignin : N ratios (25.0, 7.4 and 48.5), and water vapor holding capacities (22.7%, 25.5% and 24.6%; 24 h at 92% RH and 25 °C).
Dry litter (55 °C for 2 days) was placed in 12 × 12 cm mesh bags (gray fiberglass at the bottom, transparent polyethylene with 90% transmittance of solar radiation at the top, 1 × 1 mm openings in both materials, 2–2.5 g litter per bag).
On June 8, 2012, four sets of bags with *Avena* and *Scabiosa* litter and three sets of bags with *Calicotome* litter (less litter was available from this species) were positioned directly on the soil at each of the no-screen control areas and under each of the radiation screens (15 litterbags per set and species, 165 litter bags in total) to follow F$_{litter}$ and decay during the dry season until September 23, 2012.
To obtain a reference point for maximal fluxes, LMC and potentially active microbial biomass, two additional sets of *Avena* and *Scabiosa* litter and one set of *Calicotome* litter were deployed on June 8, 2012 in the no-screen areas only (25 litterbags in total) for sampling that were performed in the wet season.
Performance of those sets was followed until the end of the wet season in March 2013 (overall 276 days).

Samplings were performed on 4 days evenly distributed over the course of the dry season (all treatments, three sampling days for *Calicotome*): July 11, August 09, August 23, and September 23, 2012 during the dry season (*Calicotome* litter was not sampled on 23 August), and on 2 days during the wet season (no-screen control only, one sampling day for *Calicotome*): November 01, 2012 and March 11, 2013 (*Calicotome* litter was not sampled on November 11, 2013).
During the dry season, F$_{litter}$ and LMC were measured at least twice per sampling day in all treatments, using the same set of litterbags each time: once at night (0–3 h before sunrise), and once at midday (2 h before and after solar noon).
Litterbags in the no-screen control were additionally measured in the morning (2–4 h after sunrise) and in the afternoon (3–4 h after solar noon).
In the wet season (no-screen control only), F$_{litter}$ and LMC were measured at night and at midday only.
After each F$_{litter}$ (see below) and LMC measurement (bags were weighed in the field), litterbags were immediately returned to their field location.
Only after the last F$_{litter}$ measurement on each sampling day, litterbags were collected, stored in zip locked plastic bags inside a cooler, and brought back to the laboratory where they were stored at 4 °C until LMC determination and further processing during the following days.

Potentially active microbial biomass was assessed by the substrate-induced respiration (SIR) method [@Beare1990].
Litter subsamples of 0.5 g at field moisture were chopped lightly and placed in a 40-ml glass vial fitted with a septum.
The air inside the vial was flushed through two syringe needles by 400 ml of synthetic air containing 400 ppm CO$_2$ to obtain a near-ambient CO$_2$ concentration.
The incubation started with the injection of 2.5 ml glucose solution (8 g glucose/l, G-5000; Sigma Aldrich Chemical, St. Louis, MO, USA) through the septum.
After an incubation of 1–2 h at 25 °C, 0.5 ml of air was sampled from the vial headspace and injected to a gas chromatograph (Hewlett Packard 5890 gas chromatograph Series II, Santa Clara, CA, USA).
Rates of SIR were expressed as the amount of C emitted as CO$_2$ from 1 g of litter per hour.
Additional subsamples were used for determination of dry mass (at 55 °C for 2 days) and ash content (550 °C for 4 h).
For initial conditions, and the first and last dry season sampling, litter quality was determined as C and N concentrations, δ$^{13}$C and δ$^{15}$N using an isotope ratio mass spectrometer (DeltaPlus XP IRMS; Thermo Finnigan, Waltham, MA, USA), and lignin concentration determined according to @VanSoest1963.
Water vapor holding capacity of initial litter was determined by incubating 0.5 g litter (dried at 55 °C) at 92% RH and 25 °C during 24 h.
The RH value was generated over a saturated KNO$_3$ solution in a closed container [@Dirks2010].
Further details on litter quality are provided in Supporting Information.

A closed-path gas-exchange system was used in the field to measure F$_{litter}$.
The measurement chamber was constructed from a commercially available container (HPL822, 600 ml, polypropylene; Lock & Lock, Chatswood, NSW, Australia) fitted with a lid transparent to UV and PAR (90% transmittance above 300 nm; SUVT, Spartech Polycast, Stamford, CT, USA).
A small pump (WP1000, 700 ml/min flow rate; Welco Co., Tokyo, Japan) circulated the air between the chamber and the infrared gas analyzer (IRGA, LI-7500; LI-COR Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA, with the calibration tube installed; 650 ml overall volume of the system).
A thermistor (9975-019#; LI-COR), shielded against direct radiation, was placed inside the chamber to monitor chamber temperature during flux measurements.
A single litterbag was placed in the measurement chamber for 70–200 s, with the measurement starting at ambient CO$_2$ concentration.
During the day, flux measurements of litter from the no-screen and transparent treatments were conducted under the SUVT chamber lid, and measurements of litter from the radiation-block treatment under 179 Chrome orange screen + SUVT.
All parts of the system besides the measurement chamber were shielded from radiation.
Further details on the F$_{litter}$ calculations are provided in Supporting information.

Climate monitoring included hourly measurements of air temperature and RH (0.2 m above-ground, under the screens where present; HOBO, Pro v2 U23-001; Onset, Bourne, MA, USA), and of the presence of dew (ground level, next to the litterbags; 237-L Leaf Wetness Sensor; Campbell Scientific, Logan, UT, USA; one sensor of each type per treatment).
Temperature and RH were also recorded at the soil surface under the litterbags (soil–litter interface; RH only under Avena; DS1922L-F5# and DS1923, iButtons; Embedded Data Systems, Lawrenceburg, KY, USA), with two sensors per combination of treatment and species.
During each daytime CO$_2$ flux measurement, UV radiation and PAR were recorded (MU-200 and MQ-200; Apogee Instruments, Logan, UT, USA) under the same conditions as those in the measurement chamber.

## Mediterranean transect (Isreal, Italy, Spain)

On 5 days and three nights in June 2013, F$_{litter}$ and LMC were measured on freshly collected litter at all Mediterranean sites.
The measurements were performed between the night and the following afternoon during two sampling days in Israel and one sampling day in Spain (litter could not be measured in Italy at night for technical reasons).
Midday measurements solely were performed on a second day in Spain and 1 day in Italy.
In Israel and Spain, standing dead plant material of dominant winter annual species was collected [*A. sterilis* at both sites, *S. prolifera* in Israel and *Sisymbrium officinale* (L.) scop. in Spain].
In Italy, dominant, low-stature shrubs were selected, with standing dead plant material being collected from *D. pentaphyllum* [subsp. *amani* (Zohary) Ponert] and *H. italicum* (G. Don f.), freshly fallen litter being sampled from *C. monspeliensis* (L.).
We collected 9–15 samples of each litter type at night and midday, and 4–10 samples for the morning and afternoon measurements.
Litter CO$_2$ efflux was measured as described above, with the following exceptions: (i) no mesh bags were used (litter was placed directly into the measurement chamber); (ii) samples were not re-measured during the sampling day, but new litter was collected for each measurement, and for each sample, gravimetric LMC was determined in the laboratory (55 °C for 2 days); (iii) F$_{litter}$ was first measured under ambient solar radiation and then in the dark, by covering the chamber with an opaque cloth.

## Statistical analysis

In the manipulation study in Israel, we used linear regressions to assess the relationships between F$_{litter}$ and LMC, and between litter mass loss and SIR rates.
We assessed the relationship between mass loss and N mass-change using an exponential equation.
The Tukey–Kramer HSD test was used for post hoc comparisons among treatment means.
When several specific contrasts between means were tested at α = 0.05, Holm's correction was applied [@Rice1989].
We used a two-way full factorial anova for analyzing the effects of treatment and litter type on F$_{litter}$ per unit LMC and for F$_{litter}$ per unit UV irradiance.
In the Mediterranean transect, we analyzed the effect of LMC and UV irradiance on F$_{litter}$ using a multiple linear regression model, and used a linear regression to assess the relationship between F$_{litter}$ measured in the dark and LMC or temperature.
We analyzed the relation between nighttime LMC measured in the field during the dry season to the concomitant continuously recorded microclimatic variables, using a stepwise procedure (Table S1).
Similarly, daytime F$_{litter}$ was analyzed using a multiple linear regression model that included UV irradiance, time since sunrise and litter type (Table S2).
Flux densities of PAR radiation were not presented (strongly related to UV radiation, R$^2$ = 0.91) and were not used to predict daytime F$_{litter}$ (strong autocorrelation with UV radiation); instead, UV radiation was used as a predictor of F$_{litter}$.
When heterogeneity of variance occurred, data were log-transformed prior to statistical analyses.
Data were analyzed using jmp 10 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).
